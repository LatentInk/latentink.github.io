As generated media becomes easier to produce, knowing where an image came from matters. A provenance record, watermark, or disclosure can provide useful context. But the absence of a visible AI label does not establish that media is authentic, and a detection result should not be treated as a complete account of an event.

## What matters in practice

Google’s October AI roundup mentions its SynthID Detector among verification-related updates. That is a product-specific signal, not a universal test for every edited image. For an editorial site, a simpler first step is honest labeling: distinguish generated illustration, documentary photography, and a recreated diagram. Keep original sources and production notes when they matter to the claim.

## A workflow to try

1. **Identify the purpose.** Ask whether the media is decorative, explanatory, or being offered as evidence.

2. **Record the origin.** Retain source URL, creator, generation notes, or available provenance information.

3. **Verify the underlying claim.** Check the event, location, and date through other reliable evidence.

## Try this prompt

```text
Review the provenance information for [media and context].
List what the available metadata or label actually establishes,
what it does not establish, and what independent checks are needed.
Do not infer authenticity from the absence of an AI marker.
Suggest a clear editorial disclosure for the intended use.
```

## Before you rely on the result

Metadata can be lost during ordinary editing or redistribution. Detection tools also have scope and limitations. Avoid declaring a real person deceptive on the basis of a single automated signal; investigate the original context and supporting evidence.
