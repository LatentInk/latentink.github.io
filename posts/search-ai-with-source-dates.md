A search answer can be wrong even when it cites a real page. The source may describe an announcement rather than a rollout, a preview rather than general access, or an old feature under a reused product name. Current AI news is especially vulnerable because release schedules change quickly.

## What matters in practice

Google’s November 2 roundup is a useful example: it reports on October announcements. The date of the article and the date of the event are different pieces of information. When you research a tool, record both. Also distinguish whether a claim applies to the consumer app, API, enterprise product, or a limited testing program.

## A workflow to try

1. **Attach dates to claims.** Record publication date, event date, and the date you checked availability.

2. **Prefer the direct source.** Use the vendor’s release note for a release claim and keep independent testing separate.

3. **Compare conflicting reports.** Check whether different regions, plans, or rollout stages explain the disagreement.

## Try this prompt

```text
Research [current AI question] using primary sources.
For each claim, provide the direct URL, publication date,
event date if different, and relevant access conditions.
Separate announced, preview, and generally available features.
If current access cannot be verified, say so rather than guessing.
```

## Before you rely on the result

A search snippet is a lead to investigate, not a complete source. Open the page and read its conditions. When publishing, include a checked-on date and avoid calling a product “the latest” in a headline that will remain unchanged for months.
